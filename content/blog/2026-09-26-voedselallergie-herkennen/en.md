Your dog licks his paws all day. Your cat scratches herself bald behind the ears. Or the stools have been "not quite right" for months. The words *food allergy* come up quickly. Sometimes that is right – but more often something else is going on. Here is how to find out.

## What does a food allergy look like?

The most common signs are:

- **itching that lasts all year**, not just in pollen season – often on the paws, ears, muzzle, armpits or around the bottom;
- **ear or skin infections that keep coming back**;
- **digestive problems**, such as soft stools, frequent bowel movements, vomiting or a lot of wind.

None of these are unique. Fleas, an environmental allergy (to house dust mites or pollen, for example) and skin infections often look exactly the same, and they are more common. So ask your vet to rule those out first.

## Which ingredients cause problems most often?

In a food allergy the immune system nearly always reacts to a **protein** – usually an animal protein from food your pet has eaten for a long time. A large review (Mueller, Olivry & Prélaud, 2016) brought all published cases together:

- **in dogs**: mainly beef (about a third of cases), dairy, chicken and wheat;
- **in cats**: mainly beef, fish and chicken.

These ingredients top the list partly because they are in so many foods: an animal can only become allergic to something it eats. Grains such as wheat do occur, but they are clearly less often the culprit than animal proteins.

## Why a blood, hair or saliva test tells you little

Many tests promise a "food allergy profile". Research shows that blood tests, skin tests and hair or saliva tests are not reliable enough to diagnose a food allergy (Mueller & Olivry, 2017). They produce both false alarms and missed allergies.

## The only reliable way: an elimination diet

1. **Choose a food together with your vet** that contains a protein your pet has never eaten (horse, venison or insect, for example), or a veterinary diet with *hydrolysed* protein: the proteins are cut into pieces so small that the immune system usually no longer recognises them.
2. **Give nothing else.** No treats, chews, table scraps or flavoured medicines. One chicken snack can undo weeks of work.
3. **Keep it up for at least 8 weeks.** After 8 weeks, about 90% of dogs and cats with a food allergy are free of symptoms (Olivry, Mueller & Prélaud, 2015). If you stop after three weeks, you usually know nothing yet.
4. **Then put it to the test.** Go back to the old food. If the symptoms return within a few days to a few weeks, a food allergy is as good as proven. You can then reintroduce ingredients one at a time to find the exact culprit.

An elimination diet is not a matter of "just trying another brand". Many foods with lamb or salmon on the front also contain chicken, or vague terms such as "meat and animal derivatives" or "poultry". The protein your pet reacts to can hide behind those words.
