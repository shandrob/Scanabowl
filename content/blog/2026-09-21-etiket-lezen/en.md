The front of the pack tells you what the manufacturer wants you to hear. The back tells you what you need to know. Follow these six steps and you can read a label in five minutes.

## 1. The order is weight – but with the water still in

Ingredients are listed in descending order of weight, weighed **as they go into the mixer**. Fresh meat is about 70% water; dried meat (“meat meal”) hardly any. A worked example:

| Ingredient on the label | Weight when mixed | Roughly dry matter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fresh chicken | 25% | ~ 7% |
| Wheat | 15% | ~ 13% |

So “fresh chicken” at the top is no proof that the food is mostly meat. Look at the whole list: how many cereals, legumes or plant proteins follow right behind it?

## 2. Named beats vague

“Chicken” or “salmon” is precise: you know what is in there. “Meat and animal derivatives” is an official EU category that can cover anything from muscle meat to organs. By-products are not automatically bad, but you cannot see what you are feeding. With a food allergy that makes life harder.

## 3. Convert the analysis to dry matter

You cannot compare wet and dry food on the pack because wet food contains a lot of water. Convert:

**dry-matter value = value ÷ (100 − moisture) × 100**

- Wet food: 10% protein and 80% moisture → 10 ÷ 20 × 100 = **50% protein in the dry matter**.
- Dry food: 26% protein and 8% moisture → 26 ÷ 92 × 100 = **28% protein in the dry matter**.

The wet food with “only 10% protein” actually contains much more protein.

## 4. Compare per 1,000 kcal

Even fairer is comparing per unit of energy. The European FEDIAF nutrition guidelines (2021) set the minimum for adult dogs at 45–52 g protein per 1,000 kcal and for adult cats at 62.5–83 g per 1,000 kcal, depending on how active the animal is. Cats clearly need more protein than dogs.

## 5. “Complete” or “complementary”?

Only **complete** food may be your pet’s only diet. “Complementary food” (think some treats and toppers) lacks vitamins and minerals and is meant to be added to something else.

## 6. What the label does not tell you

An ingredient list says nothing about digestibility, quality control or whether the food has been tested in feeding trials. The World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA) points out that a list alone gives no assurance about the quality of the ingredients used. If in doubt, ask the manufacturer about its quality control and whether a qualified nutritionist was involved in the recipe.
