Every bag has a feeding table: "dog of 20 kg: 250–300 grams a day". Handy as a start, but that table is written for an average dog or cat. Which does not exist. A young, active border collie and a calm, neutered basset of the same weight can differ by hundreds of kilocalories a day.

## Energy does not grow in step with weight

A dog twice as heavy does not need twice as much energy. Energy needs grow more slowly, which is why animal nutritionists work with so-called metabolic body weight. The European FEDIAF guidelines use as a starting point:

- **Adult dog, moderately active**: about 110 kcal × (weight in kg)^0.75
- **Adult cat, neutered or indoors**: about 75 kcal × (weight in kg)^0.67

That looks complicated, but a few examples make it concrete:

| Animal | Energy per day (starting point) |
| --- | --- |
| Dog of 10 kg, moderately active | ± 620 kcal |
| Dog of 20 kg, moderately active | ± 1040 kcal |
| Dog of 40 kg, moderately active | ± 1750 kcal |
| Neutered indoor cat of 4 kg | ± 190 kcal |

For a 20 kg dog on a dry food with 380 kcal per 100 grams, that is about 275 grams a day. For the 4 kg cat it is roughly 220 grams of wet food with 85 kcal per 100 grams – just over two and a half 85-gram pouches.

What pushes the number up or down:

- **age**: puppies and kittens need much more per kilo, older animals often less;
- **activity**: a working dog or a dog that runs for hours every day can be far above the starting point;
- **neutering**: lowers energy needs, so an animal easily gains weight on the same portion;
- **the individual**: even animals of the same breed, weight and age differ considerably.

You don't have to work this out yourself. Enter weight, age and activity in your [pet's profile](/en/my-pet); every food page then shows how many grams of *that* food your pet needs per day.

## The scales and your hands tell you more than the table

Every calculation is a starting point. Whether the portion is right, your pet will show you. Vets use a **body condition score** for this, usually on a scale of 1 to 9. The ideal is around 4–5:

- you can **feel the ribs** easily under a thin layer of fat, without pressing;
- seen from above there is a **waist** behind the ribs;
- seen from the side the **belly tucks up** slightly towards the hind legs.

Can you barely feel the ribs, or has the waist disappeared? Then your pet is overweight. Do ribs and hip bones stick out? Then it is too thin.

## Adjust in small steps

1. Change the portion by about **10%**.
2. Weigh your pet again after **2 to 4 weeks**.
3. Repeat until the weight is stable and the condition is good.

Two more tips that make a big difference:

- **Weigh the food on kitchen scales.** A measuring cup or "a scoop" is inaccurate, especially with kibble of different sizes.
- **Count the treats.** A widely used rule of thumb is that treats and extras together should not exceed 10% of the day's calories. If you give a lot of treats, take a little off the meal.

## When to see the vet

Is your pet losing or gaining weight without any change in food, suddenly drinking much more, or are you unsure about the ideal weight? Have it checked. Losing a lot of excess weight is also best done under guidance, especially in cats: they must never lose weight too fast.
