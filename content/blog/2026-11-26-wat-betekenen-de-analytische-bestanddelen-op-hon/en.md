Turn over a bag of kibble or a tin of wet food and you will soon spot a list of percentages under the heading **analytical constituents**. Terms like "crude protein", "crude fibre" and especially "crude ash" do not sound particularly appetising and quickly raise questions. Yet these figures tell you a great deal about what actually ends up in the food bowl.

## What does the word "crude" mean?

The word "crude" has nothing to do with raw food or lower quality. It is a laboratory term referring to the standardised testing method used to measure nutrients in pet food. Under European labelling rules, manufacturers are required to list a set of core values on complete dog and cat food.

These are the main terms you will almost always come across:

- **Crude protein:** An estimate of the total amount of protein in the food, calculated from the nitrogen content measured in the laboratory. Proteins are important building blocks for muscles, organs and coat.
- **Crude fat:** The total fat content in the food. Fat is a concentrated source of energy and provides essential fatty acids that the body cannot produce on its own.
- **Crude fibre:** The portion of plant-based fibre that does not dissolve during the laboratory test. It gives an indication of the amount of indigestible fibre that plays a role in bowel movements.
- **Crude ash:** This does not mean ash has been added to the food. When a sample of food is completely burnt in the lab, the minerals remain as an ash residue — such as calcium, phosphorus, sodium and potassium.
- **Moisture:** The water content of the food. On wet food this is always listed and most of the tin or tray consists of water; in dry kibble the moisture content stays very low and does not always have to appear on the label.

## Why the percentages do not tell the whole story

A common mistake is to compare two packs blindly on a single number. On the label, the percentages are given **as fed**, meaning they include the water in the product. Suppose a tin of wet food contains 10 per cent crude protein and a kibble contains 25 per cent. At first glance, the kibble looks much higher in protein, but the wet food is mostly water. Once you discount the moisture, the actual protein content of wet food is often high.

In addition, one major group of nutrients usually does not appear on the list: **carbohydrates**, such as starches and sugars. On European pet food, it is not mandatory to list them separately. However, you can roughly estimate them by adding up the percentages of protein, fat, fibre, ash and moisture and subtracting that from 100 per cent.

Finally, a percentage says nothing about **quality and digestibility**. Two foods can have the exact same percentage of crude protein, while one contains mostly highly digestible animal proteins and the other contains a lot of plant-based proteins with a different amino acid profile. The same goes for minerals: the total crude ash figure does not show whether the balance between them — such as between calcium and phosphorus — meets the FEDIAF nutritional guidelines.

## Practical checklist for reading the figures

Want to use the analytical constituents to assess pet food better? Run through these points:

- **Check the moisture content first:** Never compare wet food and dry food directly using the numbers on the label without taking the water percentage into account.
- **Combine the figures with the ingredients list:** Use the ingredients to see where the protein and fat come from, and the figures to check the proportions.
- **Look out for separately listed minerals:** Sometimes calcium, phosphorus or omega fatty acids are listed separately below the list. This is especially useful when looking at food for a specific life stage, such as a growing puppy or an older cat.
- **Do not forget the bigger picture:** A higher percentage of protein or fat is not automatically better; what is suitable depends on your pet's age, weight and activity level.
