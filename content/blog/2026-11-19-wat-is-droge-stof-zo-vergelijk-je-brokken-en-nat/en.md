If you look at the back of a tin of wet food, you might be surprised by the low protein percentage: it often says just 8% or 10%. On a bag of kibble, you can easily read 25% or 30% crude protein. Yet that does not mean kibble is automatically higher in protein, because the difference is almost entirely down to the water.

## What dry matter actually means

Everything in a food bowl consists of two parts: **moisture** (water) and **dry matter**. Dry matter is simply everything that remains if you were to remove all the water from the food. Think of the proteins, fats, carbohydrates, fibre, vitamins and minerals.

On European labels for dog and cat food, the **analytical constituents** are listed as the food sits in the packaging. Because wet food consists mostly of water (often around 75% to 82%), all the other percentages are, in effect, diluted. Dry food, by contrast, contains very little moisture, usually around 8% to 10%. As a result, the numbers on a bag of kibble look much higher on paper.

## A simple calculation example

If you want to compare two types of food fairly side by side, you first need to take the moisture out of the equation. That sounds complicated, but all you need is a quick division sum.

Suppose you have a tin of wet food with **8% crude protein** and **80% moisture** on the label. That means 20% of the tin consists of dry matter (100% minus 80%). To find out how much protein is in that dry matter, divide the protein percentage by the dry matter percentage and multiply by 100:

- **Wet food:** 8 divided by 20, times 100 = **40% protein on a dry-matter basis**.

Now compare that with a bag of kibble that states **26% crude protein** and **10% moisture**. Here, 90% of the kibble is dry matter (100% minus 10%). The same calculation gives:

- **Kibble:** 26 divided by 90, times 100 = **roughly 29% protein on a dry-matter basis**.

In this example, your dog or cat therefore gets proportionally more protein from the wet food than from the kibble, even though the label initially seemed to say the opposite. For this exact reason, the nutritional guidelines from **FEDIAF** (the European pet food industry federation) also work with values based on dry matter or energy. That is the only way to measure different types of food by the same yardstick.

## What to look out for in practice

Of course, you do not need to get out a calculator for every product when you are shopping. However, it helps to recognise a few pitfalls when looking at packaging:

- **Do not compare apples with oranges:** never put wet food and kibble directly side by side based on the raw percentages on the back.
- **Watch out for a missing moisture percentage:** with dry food, the moisture content is sometimes not stated on the packaging, because in the EU it often does not need to be declared if it is below 14%. For kibble, you can usually calculate using roughly 10% moisture (so 90% dry matter).
- **Look beyond protein alone:** convert crude fat, crude fibre and crude ash (minerals) in the same way if you want to make targeted comparisons between foods.
- **Remember that moisture counts too:** for cats and dogs that drink little, the water in wet food is not useless filler, but a welcome contribution to their daily fluid intake.

## Quick checklist for your food choice

Want to quickly check for yourself how two products compare? Follow these steps:

- Find the moisture percentage on the label (if it is not listed on kibble, use roughly 10%).
- Subtract the moisture percentage from 100: that is the dry matter proportion.
- Divide the percentage of your chosen constituent (for example crude protein or crude fat) by the dry matter proportion and multiply by 100.
- Only then compare the results of one food with the other.
