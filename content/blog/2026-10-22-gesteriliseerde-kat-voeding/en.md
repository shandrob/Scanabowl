Spaying or neutering is a sensible choice for most cats. But there is one side effect many owners are not prepared for: the extra weight. A neutered cat that keeps eating exactly what she ate before often gains noticeably within a few months. Why is that?

## Burning less, wanting more

Two things change at the same time.

- **Energy needs go down.** In an American study of twenty cats, the energy that female cats needed to stay at the same weight was clearly lower 8 and 16 weeks after spaying than before (Hoenig & Ferguson, 2002). Many cats also become a little calmer after the operation.
- **Appetite goes up.** A large review (Vendramini et al., 2020) describes that cats and dogs eat more after the operation – and that this increase starts within just a few days.

Needing less and wanting more: that is exactly the combination that leads to overweight. And overweight is not harmless in cats; among other things it increases the risk of diabetes and joint problems.

## How much less?

The European FEDIAF guidelines use about 100 kcal × (weight in kg)^0.67 per day for an active adult cat, and about **75 kcal** for a neutered or indoor cat – a quarter less. For a 4 kg cat that is about 190 kcal a day instead of just over 250.

That is a starting point. Whether it is right for your cat, you can tell from her weight and body condition: you should be able to feel the ribs easily, and seen from above there is a slight waist.

## What to do differently from day one

1. **Reduce the portion straight after the operation**, not once your cat has already gained weight. Losing weight is much harder for cats than not gaining it.
2. **Weigh the food.** Judging by eye almost always goes wrong.
3. **No full bowl all day.** Spread the daily portion over several small meals. A puzzle feeder or snuffle mat makes your cat work for her food.
4. **Play every day.** A few short play sessions help against boredom and burn energy.
5. **Weigh your cat every few weeks** in the first months, and adjust the portion in steps of about 10%.

## Do you need special "sterilised" food?

Not necessarily. "Sterilised" is not a legal term. Such foods usually have a somewhat lower energy density, sometimes more fibre or a formula aimed at the urinary tract. That can help, because your cat can then eat a slightly bigger portion for the same calories. But any complete food works if you feed the right amount. Wet food has the added advantage of lots of water and fewer calories per gram – see also [why wet food is more than a treat for cats](/en/blog/2026-09-28-natvoer-kat-water).
