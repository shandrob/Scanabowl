Cooking for your dog or cat yourself feels completely natural: you know exactly what goes into the bowl and you pick out fresh ingredients. Yet in practice, it turns out to be quite difficult to serve up a complete and balanced menu every day this way. Whereas a person easily varies between dozens of foods, a pet often gets the same few recipes for months on end.

## Why meat and vegetables alone are not enough

A portion of cooked chicken with rice and carrot looks healthy, but is missing several crucial nutrients. According to the guidelines from **FEDIAF** (the European federation for pet food), dogs and cats need dozens of essential nutrients every day in the right proportions to one another: proteins, fats, vitamins and minerals.

A well-known sticking point with home cooking is the ratio between **calcium and phosphorus**. Muscle meat contains plenty of phosphorus, but barely any calcium. If an animal gets too little calcium over a longer period, the body can start making up that shortfall from the bones. Especially in growing puppies and kittens, that balance is particularly critical.

In addition, dogs and cats differ greatly in what they can process. Dogs can make good use of plant-based ingredients such as cooked potato, rice or vegetables alongside animal proteins. Cats are **obligate carnivores** (true meat-eaters): they depend on animal tissues for certain nutrients, such as taurine and vitamin A from animal sources, which they cannot produce in sufficient amounts from plants themselves.

## What changes during cooking

Heating makes starch and some vegetables easier to digest and kills harmful bacteria that can be present in raw meat. At the same time, cooking affects nutritional value. Water-soluble vitamins, such as various B vitamins, can be partly lost through heat or wash away when you drain the cooking water.

Watch out for **cooked bones** as well: never give them to your dog or cat. Heating makes bone brittle and liable to splinter sharply, which is dangerous for the oesophagus and stomach. On a cooked diet, calcium must therefore always be supplemented via a suitable calcium source, not through cooked chew bones or chicken bones.

## Supplementing or replacing completely

It makes a big difference whether you occasionally cook something as an extra treat, or switch entirely to home-made meals.

Do you want to mix a spoonful of cooked pumpkin, a piece of white fish or some lean chicken into the dry food a few times a week as a flavour enhancer? As long as such extras remain a small part of the daily portion, the complete food still provides the foundation. Vets generally advise keeping treats and toppings limited so that you do not upset the nutritional balance of the main food.

Do you want to cook **every day and completely** yourself? Then you need a precisely calculated recipe, including the right dosage of a suitable vitamin and mineral supplement. General recipes from the internet often do not match the needs of your individual pet well. A **veterinary nutrition specialist** (a vet who specialises in nutrition) can draw up a tailored recipe based on age, weight and health.

## Practical checklist before you put the pan on

Are you getting started in the kitchen for your dog or cat? Keep these points in mind:

- **Avoid toxic kitchen ingredients:** onion, garlic, leek, chives, grapes, raisins, chocolate, macadamia nuts and products containing xylitol never belong in the food bowl.
- **Cook without salt, stock or herbs and spices:** human flavourings put unnecessary strain on the body and sometimes contain hidden onion or garlic powder.
- **Do not give cooked bones:** for a complete cooked diet, only use a safe, measured calcium supplement according to a specialist recipe.
- **Weigh ingredients accurately:** small discrepancies in fat, meat or supplements add up over the long term, especially for small dogs and cats.
- **Keep the cooking water:** if you gently poach meat or vegetables in a little water, you can serve the cooled liquid as well so that leached nutrients are retained.
