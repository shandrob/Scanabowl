"Grain-free" appears on more and more bags, often in large letters. It sounds more natural and healthier. But is it? The short answer: grain-free says little about how good a food is. The long answer is below.

## Grain-free is not the same as low-carb

Kibble needs starch to hold together. When the grains come out, something else goes in: usually **potato, sweet potato, peas, lentils, chickpeas or tapioca**. A grain-free food can therefore contain as much – or even more – carbohydrate than a food with rice or oats.

So don't look at the word on the front; look at the ingredient list. If three kinds of legumes or potato are high on the list, they are the basis of the food. Scanabowl estimates the carbohydrate content on a dry-matter basis for every food, so you can compare.

## Are grains bad for dogs?

Not for most dogs. During domestication dogs became better at digesting starch: on average they have many more copies of the gene for amylase – the enzyme that breaks down starch – than wolves (Axelsson et al., 2013). Properly cooked grains are well digestible for a healthy dog.

## What about allergy?

Grain allergy exists, but it is less common than often assumed. In a large review of published cases in dogs, wheat was involved in about 13% of food allergies and maize in about 4%. Beef (34%), dairy and chicken were more common (Mueller, Olivry & Prélaud, 2016). If your dog has a proven wheat allergy, a food without wheat makes sense. Without an allergy, there is no reason to avoid grains. Read more about recognising a food allergy in [this article](/en/blog/2026-09-26-voedselallergie-herkennen).

## The FDA heart investigation

In July 2018 the US FDA began investigating reports of **dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM)**, a disease of the heart muscle, in dogs – including breeds not normally prone to it. Many of those dogs ate food with a lot of peas, lentils or potato high in the ingredient list, often sold as grain-free.

In December 2022 the FDA said the reports it had collected were not enough evidence to establish a causal link between any particular type of food and DCM, and that it would only give further updates once there is meaningful new scientific information. Scientists are still researching the question.

What does that mean for you? There is no reason to panic, but no reason to see grain-free food as "safer" either. If your dog has a heart condition or belongs to a breed predisposed to one, discuss the choice of food with your vet.

## And cats?

Cats are true carnivores and need few carbohydrates. But for cats too, grain-free does not automatically mean low in carbohydrates. A wet food with plenty of meat and little filler usually scores better for a cat than a grain-free kibble full of potato. What matters most for cats, you can read in [why wet food is more than a treat for cats](/en/blog/2026-09-28-natvoer-kat-water).

## What should you look for?

- **Where does the protein come from?** A clearly named meat at the top is better than "animal derivatives", and animal protein is better than protein from peas or maize.
- **Is it a complete food?** Only then can it be the whole meal.
- **Are the energy and the portion right** for your pet?
