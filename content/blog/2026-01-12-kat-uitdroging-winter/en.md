The Christmas tree is gone, New Year’s resolutions are underway, and it might even be freezing outside. Inside, the heating is turned right up. Lovely for us, and your cat probably loves curling up right next to that radiator too.

But there is an invisible danger lurking in this dry winter air: low fluid intake.

This week we are not looking at what your cat eats, but at how wet it is. Because did you know that a cat’s total fluid balance depends much more on her food bowl than on her water bowl?

## Your house tiger’s “desert heritage”

To understand why this goes wrong, we have to travel back in time. Our domestic cat descends from *Felis silvestris lybica*, the African wildcat – a desert animal.

Evolution programmed cats with a much weaker thirst drive than ours (or dogs’). In the wild, cats got most of their moisture from their prey (a mouse is around 70–75% water). So they hardly ever needed to drink separately.

At home we often give them dry kibble, which contains at most 10% moisture. Your cat then suddenly has to drink far more from a bowl to make up the difference. But her body does not send that signal strongly enough.

## What does the science say?

Nutritional research has long shown that cats fed exclusively on dry food, even with unlimited fresh drinking water available, take in less water overall than cats fed wet food or moisture-rich diets. In a study by **Buckley et al. (2011, *British Journal of Nutrition*)**, cats on a high-moisture diet (around 73% moisture) had a significantly higher total water intake and produced a larger volume of less concentrated urine than on the same diet in dry form.

The conclusion: a cat usually does not drink enough extra water to make up the moisture difference between dry kibble and wet food. Especially in cats prone to urinary crystals or lower urinary tract problems, extra moisture in the diet helps dilute the urine.

## What to look out for in winter

Want to support your cat’s fluid intake? Keep an eye on these points:

### ✅ What helps

We look for natural ways to get more moisture into the daily menu:

- **Complete wet food (tins, trays or pouches):** wet food is 75 to 80% water. Even a single portion of wet food a day alongside kibble noticeably increases total water intake.
- **Unsalted broth or cooking liquid from chicken or fish:** a few tablespoons of lukewarm cooking liquid (without salt, onion or seasoning) over the food makes extra drinking appealing.
- **Moisture-rich grated vegetables (such as a tiny bit of cooked courgette):** high in water and low in calories, and readily accepted by some cats when mixed into wet food.

### ❌ What to watch out for

- **Unnecessarily high salt (sodium) levels:** extra salt does stimulate drinking, but high-sodium diets should only be used when specifically prescribed by a vet for certain bladder stones.
- **Relying solely on “urinary” claims on dry kibble:** cranberry or herbal extracts on the label never replace the most important aid for the bladder: plenty of moisture.

## Practical tip for this week

Does your cat strictly eat kibble and refuse wet food? Try the “Winter Soup” method.

Add a tablespoon of lukewarm water (not ice-cold!) to the kibble in the morning and evening. Let it soak for 2 minutes so the aroma is released. Many cats love the “gravy” that forms.

If your cat won’t touch it, place several wide water bowls in quiet spots around the house – and never right next to the food bowl or the litter tray. Instinctively, cats prefer to drink in a separate place.
