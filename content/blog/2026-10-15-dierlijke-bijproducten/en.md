On many packs it comes first: **meat and animal derivatives**. To many owners that sounds like leftover slaughterhouse waste – beaks, feathers, hooves. That picture is wrong. But the term is not entirely innocent either. It is different from what you might think.

## What is allowed in pet food?

In the European Union it is strictly regulated which animal by-products may end up in animal feed (Regulation (EC) No 1069/2009). By-products are divided into three categories by risk. Only the lowest-risk category may be used in pet food: **Category 3**.

It includes parts of animals that were **passed fit for human consumption** at slaughter but are not sold as human food for commercial reasons. Think of heart, liver, kidneys, lungs, stomach and tripe: parts that are rarely eaten in many Western countries, but are a normal meal in other cuisines.

## Organ meat is actually nutritious

For a dog or cat these parts are anything but worthless. A wild cat eats her prey whole, organs included. And those organs are full of nutrients:

- **liver** is rich in vitamin A, iron and B vitamins;
- **heart** contains a lot of taurine – essential for cats;
- **tripe and stomach** provide protein and are a favourite of many dogs.

A food with a good share of named organ meat can therefore be perfectly fine.

## The real problem: you don't know what it is

Where it goes wrong is the **name**. The law allows pet food manufacturers to use a **category name** instead of each individual ingredient, such as "meat and animal derivatives", "fish and fish derivatives" or "derivatives of vegetable origin" (Regulation (EC) No 767/2009, Annex VII). Anything can hide behind that one name:

- **which species?** Chicken, beef, pork, lamb – all allowed, and it may vary from batch to batch;
- **which part?** Muscle meat, organ or, for example, connective tissue;
- **how much of which animal?** "Meat and animal derivatives (of which 4% beef)" means the rest of that group can be something other than beef – while the front of the pack may say "with beef".

For most animals this is mainly a question of transparency. For an animal with a **food allergy** it is a real problem: you cannot rule out that it contains exactly the chicken or beef your pet reacts to.

## In short

- "Animal derivatives" in European pet food are not waste: they are parts of approved slaughter animals, often nutritious organ meat.
- The drawback is the **vague name**: you don't know which animal or which part.
- Where possible, choose a food that **names** the species and the part – especially if your pet has an allergy.
