The muzzle turns grey, the walks get shorter and the naps longer. Time for senior food? Maybe. But "senior" on the bag says less than you would think, and dogs and cats age in very different ways.

## When is a pet a senior?

There is no fixed limit. As a rule of thumb:

- **dogs**: large breeds from about 6–7 years, small breeds only from 9–10 years;
- **cats**: from about 10–11 years.

More important than age is how your pet feels and looks.

## "Senior food" is not a fixed standard

There is no legal definition and no separate FEDIAF standard for senior food. A food called "senior" has to meet the same standards as any complete food for adult animals. What the manufacturer does differently varies widely: one senior food contains fewer calories, another more protein, a third somewhat less phosphorus or added ingredients for the joints. So always compare the label, not just the word on the front.

## Older dogs: often need less energy

Many older dogs move less and therefore need less energy. The FEDIAF guidelines use a lower energy requirement for dogs over seven than for young adult dogs. The biggest risk for an older dog is therefore **overweight** – which strains the joints at exactly the moment they become more vulnerable.

Less energy does not mean less protein. Older dogs in particular lose muscle easily. A review on nutrition for older dogs and cats advises continuing to give older dogs enough protein (Laflamme, 2005). So reduce the portion, not the quality.

## Older cats: sometimes need more

Cats are different. Around middle age overweight is the biggest risk, but in older cats – from about 12 years – energy needs often rise again. Some older cats digest fat and protein less well, so they have to eat more to absorb the same (Laflamme, 2005).

That is why, for older cats:

- **Weight loss is often the first sign of disease.** Weigh your cat regularly; a 4 kg cat that loses 300 grams loses almost 8% of her weight.
- **Keeping muscle matters.** In older cats, loss of muscle mass is linked to a shorter life. So don't restrict protein on your own.
- **Kidney problems are common** in older cats. A kidney diet can make a big difference then, but only after tests and on your vet's advice.

## Practical tips for every senior

- **Have your pet checked more often**, for example twice a year. Many age-related diseases are treatable when found early.
- **Watch the teeth.** A pet that eats poorly is sometimes simply in pain. Wet food or soaked kibble can help.
- **Keep track of weight** and adjust the portion in small steps.
- **Water**: make sure fresh water is always available, and for cats preferably in several places.
