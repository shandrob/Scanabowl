Cats descend from desert animals and naturally drink little. In the wild a cat got most of her water from her prey, which is roughly three-quarters water. A dry kibble contains about ten percent moisture at most. A cat on dry food would have to drink far more to get the same result – and usually she doesn't.

## What research shows

A UK study (Buckley et al., 2011, *British Journal of Nutrition*) fed cats diets with different moisture contents. On the food with 73% moisture, urine specific gravity – a measure of how concentrated the urine is – was clearly lower than on diets with 53% moisture or less. The cats drank less water from the bowl on the wet food, but their **total** fluid intake was higher. And the relative supersaturation for calcium oxalate, one of the minerals that form bladder stones, came close to “undersaturated” on the wettest diet.

In plain words: more moisture in the food means more dilute urine, and more dilute urine gives crystals less chance to form.

## Wet food also matches what cats seem to want

Research (Hewson-Hughes et al., 2011, *Journal of Experimental Biology*) showed that cats that can choose compose a diet with roughly 52% of energy from protein, 36% from fat and only 12% from carbohydrates. Wet food usually sits much closer to that than dry food, which often contains a lot more starch because of the way it is made.

## Three ways to give your cat more moisture

1. **Feed at least some wet food.** Even half a portion a day helps. Build up slowly over 7–10 days.
2. **Moisten dry food.** Add a tablespoon of lukewarm water and let it soak for two minutes. Many cats love the “gravy”.
3. **Put out several water bowls,** in quiet places and not next to the food bowl or litter tray.

## A word of caution

Has your cat ever had urinary crystals, bladder stones or kidney problems? Then discuss with your vet which food is right. In that case a therapeutic diet is sometimes needed.
